Research Interest Summary

Computing Education

Anecdotally, I have noticed many of my peers hesitate when I recommend upper-division electives, with many of them questioning their ability to succeed in those courses, typically concerned about the mathematical rigor or large projects. These experiences have motivated my interest in discovering and alleviating barriers to success, both individual and societal, in computing. I'm particularly curious about how psychological factors — such as sense of belonging, self-efficacy, and impostor syndrome — manifest in computing students. My goal is to use qualitative methods to identify and understand how students experience computing, especially students pursuing less normative paths. For instance, why do so many students pursue software engineering or adjacent careers? What effect does this convergence to these careers as a "norm" have on what and how students learn in their computing courses? Is this "norm" discouraging students from studying other aspects of computing that might be perceived as less relevant to a software engineering career, such as theoretical computer science, architecture, or human-computer interaction? I intend for my research in this area to understand how we can incorporate student sentiments, boost engagement, foster curiosity, and broaden participation in these disciplines of computing.


Human-AI Interaction

In the first year of my undergraduate studies, I wrote a paper analyzing how in the world of Fahrenheit 451, an excessive dependence on technology stripped humans of their individuality and their curiosity, turning them into mindless cogs in a machine. I believe the rapid development of generative AI poses a similar threat if left unchecked — generative AI has emerged as an incredibly disruptive technology, one that has influenced how we communicate, learn, and work in many facets of life. For that reason, it is important to understand how humans are adapting and responding to its integration, especially in educational spaces. Do teachers see it as a productivity tool or as an obstacle to effective pedagogy? How can we foster AI literacy among students to use generative AI in ways that support, rather than hinder, their learning? What opportunities and challenges exist with generative AI integration in educational spaces? What drives humans' fear that generative AI will make their roles obsolete? I am especially interested in understanding how we can mitigate the negative effects of cognitive offloading and integrate generative AI in effective ways that enhance human capabilities instead of replacing them.


Algorithm Design

While I am broadly interested in computer science theory as a whole, I have taken a particular interest to designing algorithms made for specific input cases and computing paradigms. For example, if we know that our input array is nearly sorted, algorithms like Insertion Sort can achieve better performance than algorithms like Merge Sort. I am interested in designing algorithms that abide by similar principles — given these restrictions about our input or allowed resources, can we design an efficient algorithm that works better than a general, all-purpose algorithm and verify its performance experimentally? I am also interested in understanding how we can utilize alternative computing paradigms, such as parallel or quantum computing, to provide better performance beyond what a classical, single-processor algorithm is capable of. However, for me, it's not enough to simply design the algorithm and prove its runtime and correctness — I believe that it's important to describe the process and explain the mechanisms to other individuals in an easily digestible manner, to effectively communicate the insights that motivated to the discovery, in ways that elevate our understanding of the problem.

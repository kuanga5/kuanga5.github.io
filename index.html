Research Interest Summary

Computing Education

Anecdotally, I have noticed many of my peers hesitate when I recommend upper-division electives, with many of them questioning their ability to succeed in those courses, typically concerned about the mathematical rigor or large projects. These experiences have motivated my interest in discovering and alleviating barriers to success, both individual and societal, in computing. I'm particularly curious about how psychological factors — such as sense of belonging, self-efficacy, and impostor syndrome — manifest in computing students. My goal is to use qualitative methods to identify and understand how students experience computing, especially students pursuing less normative paths. For instance, why do so many students pursue software engineering or adjacent careers? What effect does this convergence to these careers as a "norm" have on what and how students learn in their computing courses? Is this "norm" discouraging students from studying other aspects of computing that might be perceived as less relevant to a software engineering career, such as theoretical computer science, architecture, or human-computer interaction? I intend for my research in this area to understand how we can incorporate student sentiments, boost engagement, foster curiosity, and broaden participation in computing.


Algorithm Design

While I am broadly interested in computer science theory as a whole, I have taken a particular interest to designing algorithms that not only achieve good theoretical bounds but can also be implemented in practice. This includes verifying our algorithms' theoretical bounds and comparing them against state-of-the-art algorithms via experimental analysis. With regard to algorithm design techniques, I am primarily intrigued by reductions and the application of subroutines as building blocks for a larger algorithm. For example, I am fascinated by the applications of Grover's algorithm to achieve speedups in hybrid quantum-classical algorithms and the applications of prefix sum in parallel algorithm design. Formulating problems in ways that make existing subroutines useful opens up new ways to design efficient algorithms while simultaneously simplifying the analysis.
